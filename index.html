English Language Learners, School Type and Per-Pupil Spending

by Mia Zajec

Updated April 2026

for Data Visualization for All
with Prof. Jack Dougherty
Trinity College, Hartford CT, USA

Intro

English Language Learners (ELLs) account for 8.8% of enrolled students in Connecticut. The term refers to students who are learning English in addition to their primary language. This project explores two questions about ELL students in Connecticut: how their enrollment is distributed across different school types and how district-level ELL enrollment relates to per-pupil spending.

For the first analysis, I examined the eight Connecticut towns with the highest overall student enrollment and compared the distribution of ELL students across traditional public, magnet, charter, program, and other school types. For the second, I compared the percentage of ELL students enrolled in selected school districts around Hartford with each district's per-pupil spending. The results show that traditional public schools account for the largest share of ELL enrollment among the towns examined. The spending analysis shows a slight negative relationship between ELL enrollment percentage and per-pupil spending among the districts included in the dataset. These findings are descriptive and should be interpreted alongside the data limitations discussed below.

Findings

SCHOOL TYPE

Of the eight towns I researched, traditional public schools had the largest share of English Language Learner enrollment. The stacked bar chart below breaks down ELL enrollment in each town by school type. The "Other" category combines several less common school types, including college-affiliated schools, Connecticut technical education and career schools, and Open Choice programs. Looking across the eight towns, traditional public schools consistently account for the largest portion of ELL enrollment, while the other school types make up smaller shares. This shows that, within the towns included in my analysis, ELL students are primarily enrolled in traditional public schools.

Figure 1: Explore the bar chart, comparing English Language Learner enrollment across school types in the eight Connecticut towns with the highest overall student enrollment for the 2021-2022 school year.

PER-PUPIL SPENDING

For the second part of my analysis, I created a scatterplot to compare the percentage of English Language Learners enrolled in each district with per-pupil spending. ELL enrollment percentage is shown on the horizontal axis, while per-pupil spending is shown on the vertical axis. Each point represents a different school district, and hovering over a point shows the district name, per-pupil spending, and percentage of ELL students enrolled. The trend line shows a slight negative relationship between the two variables, meaning that districts with higher percentages of ELL students tended to have somewhat lower per-pupil spending within the data I analyzed. Most of the districts had ELL enrollment between 0% and 10%, while per-pupil spending generally ranged from about $12,000 to $25,000. Although the chart shows a pattern between these two variables, it does not tell us what causes the differences in spending between districts.

Figure 2: Explore the scatterplot, comparing English Language Learner enrollment percentages with per-pupil spending by district for the 2021-2022 school year.

For this project, I used Connecticut's official education data website to find the information needed for both analyses. The original datasets included more information than I needed, so I cleaned and organized the data before creating the visualizations. There were also some limitations in the available data. For the school type analysis, not every town had data available for every school category, meaning some categories are missing for certain towns. This can affect how the percentages compare across towns. Similarly, some districts in the spending analysis were missing data, which may affect the overall trend shown in the scatterplot. Because of these limitations, the charts are meant to show general patterns in the available data rather than provide definitive conclusions about ELL enrollment or school funding.
